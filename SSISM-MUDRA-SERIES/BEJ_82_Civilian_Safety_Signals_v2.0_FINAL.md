### Abstract

This paper proposes a structured framework for community communication and civilian safety signalling in displacement and emergency contexts where verbal communication may be difficult, unreliable, or temporarily unavailable.
Developed within the Systemic Structural Intelligence Safety Management (SSISM) framework, the proposal examines how a small, teachable vocabulary of fifteen hand signals could support the communication of protective needs, requests for assistance, uncertainty, resource status, and safety-related information.

The framework is guided by five distinctions:
A perceived signal is not necessarily a confirmed fact.
A warning is not proof that a particular hazard exists.
A reported safe location is not a guarantee of continuing safety.
A communication signal does not independently authorize a hazardous action.
A formally specified system is not equivalent to an empirically validated system.
The proposed architecture separates signal recognition, interpretation, decision authorization, action, and subsequent review. It also addresses conflicting messages, accessibility, communication failure, and the risks of false reassurance.
The fifteen proposed signals are intended as a research and educational vocabulary, not as a substitute for official emergency instructions, trained humanitarian personnel, professional explosive-ordnance risk education, or context-specific evacuation procedures.

The governing principle is:
Perception → Signal Recognition → Contextual Verification → Authorized Protective Response → Review
The framework makes no quantitative claim about casualty reduction, operational effectiveness, or predictive accuracy. Those outcomes require appropriate testing and independent evaluation.
Its central contribution is a formal attempt to make uncertainty visible, protect civilian dignity, and prevent an unverified communication signal from being mistaken for proof of safety.
Keywords: SSISM, community intelligence, civilian protection, displacement, safety signalling, epistemic verification, human oversight, accessibility, emergency communication, research integrity.

### I. Introduction and Problem Statement

Communities experiencing displacement, natural disasters, armed conflict, or interruptions to ordinary communication may encounter situations in which people cannot reliably hear, understand, or transmit spoken information.
Children, older people, people with disabilities, caregivers, and people unfamiliar with the local language may experience additional barriers.
A shared visual vocabulary could help communicate a limited number of immediate needs. However, the existence of a gesture does not establish that it will be understood consistently or that the resulting action will be safe.
The research problem is therefore not simply how to create more signals.
It is:
How can a community communication framework reduce ambiguity without creating false confidence, excluding vulnerable people, or encouraging unsafe decisions?
BEJ 82 addresses this question by proposing a structured vocabulary of fifteen civilian safety signals and a verification architecture for interpreting them.
The paper does not claim that the proposed gestures are internationally standardized, universally recognizable, or suitable for every emergency. Their meanings must be taught, tested, and adapted in consultation with the people expected to use them.

1.1 Research objectives

This paper has five objectives.
Define a consistent relationship between a proposed signal and its intended communicative meaning.
Separate the observation of a signal from the interpretation of its meaning.
Specify how uncertainty, conflicting messages, and communication failure should be represented.
Establish civilian dignity, accessibility, and human oversight as design requirements.
Define a future research pathway for testing comprehension, usability, and safety.

1.2 Scope boundaries

This is a civilian communication and safety-design proposal.
It is not:
A military hand-signal manual.
A reconnaissance or surveillance procedure.
A guide to evading detection.
A substitute for official evacuation or shelter instructions.
A method for identifying enemy intentions.
A certified mine-safety or explosive-ordnance procedure.
Evidence that a particular movement, shelter, or route is safe.
The framework is intended to support protective communication rather than combat activity.
II. The SSISM Epistemic Foundation
SSISM begins with the principle:
Reality Before Narrative.
A system must distinguish what has been observed from what has been inferred, reported, calculated, or left unknown.
In civilian safety communication, this distinction is essential because people may need to act under uncertainty without possessing complete information.

2.1 Five epistemic categories

Every significant claim within the proposed system should be classified using the following taxonomy.
Category
Definition
Example

OBSERVED
Directly perceived information
A person visibly raises a recognized hand signal.

SOURCED
Information attributed to an identifiable source
An authorized emergency service issues an instruction.

CALCULATED
A result derived from explicit inputs and a reproducible method
A resource inventory shows that three water containers remain.

INFERRED
An interpretation derived from available information
A person may be requesting assistance.

UNKNOWN
Information that has not been adequately established
Whether an unfamiliar building is safe to enter.
These categories describe the status of information, not its automatic truth or reliability.
For example, observing a gesture may be a reliable observation. Interpreting that gesture correctly remains a separate question.
Likewise, a message can be accurately attributed to a source without the underlying claim necessarily being correct.

2.2 Four formal distinctions

The framework adopts the following rules:
[ \text{Observed Signal} \neq \text{Confirmed Meaning} ]
[ \text{Reported Safety} \neq \text{Verified Safety} ]
[ \text{Capability} \neq \text{Authorization} ]
[ \text{Formal Specification} \neq \text{Empirical Validation} ]
These distinctions are not decorative statements. They establish boundaries on what the system is permitted to conclude.

2.3 Uncertainty must remain visible

When the meaning of a signal is uncertain, the system must not silently convert uncertainty into a confident interpretation.
When two signals conflict, the system must not assume that the most recent, most forceful, or most confident signal is necessarily correct.
When no reliable conclusion can be reached, the system should record the uncertainty and defer to established emergency guidance or an appropriate human decision-maker.
This is the epistemic foundation of the proposed framework.

### III. System Architecture

The proposed architecture contains six stages.

Stage 1 — Observation
A person perceives an event, message, or communication signal.

Stage 2 — Recognition
The person or receiving system identifies the proposed signal, if possible.

Stage 3 — Interpretation
The recognized signal is interpreted according to the community's previously taught definitions.

Stage 4 — Verification and authorization
The relevant information is checked against available context, known limitations, and applicable emergency instructions.

Stage 5 — Protective response
An appropriate response is selected by the responsible person or authority, taking account of immediate danger, accessibility, and established safety procedures.

Stage 6 — Review
The group or responsible coordinator checks whether the message was understood, whether the response remains appropriate, and whether new information changes the decision.
The conceptual sequence is:
[ O \rightarrow R \rightarrow I \rightarrow V \rightarrow A \rightarrow E ]
Where:
(O) = Observation
(R) = Recognition
(I) = Interpretation
(V) = Verification and authorization
(A) = Action
(E) = Evaluation
The sequence is a conceptual model, not a claim that every emergency can be handled through a slow, strictly linear process. Immediate danger may require prompt protective action under established emergency instructions.

3.1 A formal communication rule
Let:
(g) denote a proposed gesture;
(r) denote the receiver's recognition of that gesture;
(m) denote its intended meaning;
(c) denote the relevant context;
(a) denote a proposed response.
A basic communication rule can be written as:
[ g \rightarrow r \rightarrow \widehat{m} ]
Here, (\widehat{m}) represents the receiver's interpretation of the intended meaning.
It does not guarantee that the interpretation is correct.
The next decision is:
[ D(r,\widehat{m},c) \rightarrow a ]
where (D) represents a human-supervised decision process that considers the signal, its interpretation, the context, and applicable safety requirements.
The function (D) is not yet a validated algorithm. Its decision rules remain a subject for research.

3.2 Failure must be an explicit system state
The framework recognizes at least five communication states:
RECOGNIZED: A signal appears to have been identified.
UNCERTAIN: Its meaning cannot be established confidently.
CONFLICTING: Two or more signals communicate incompatible meanings.
UNAVAILABLE: The intended recipient cannot see or receive the signal.
WITHDRAWN OR SUPERSEDED: The original sender or an authorized coordinator clarifies or replaces a previous message.
These states must not be collapsed into a single binary distinction between success and failure.
An unrecognized signal is not evidence that no danger exists. Silence is not evidence of safety. A lack of disagreement is not proof of agreement.

4.1 Why the vocabulary is deliberately limited
The objective is not to create a complex secret language.
A community vocabulary should minimize ambiguity, support accessible learning, and avoid creating a false impression that every emergency can be managed through a predetermined set of gestures.
Fifteen proposed entries are a design choice, not an empirically established optimum.
Future testing may demonstrate that fewer signals, alternative signals, or different teaching methods would be more effective.

4.2 Gesture design requirements
Before a gesture is included in a practical teaching programme, it should be assessed for:
Distinguishability from other gestures.
Comprehension without verbal explanation.
Suitability for people with different physical abilities.
Visibility under realistic but safe teaching conditions.
Cultural interpretation and unintended meanings.
Ease of learning and later recall.
Potential confusion with established local or humanitarian signals.
The framework does not assume that large movements, visual signals, or hand gestures will be usable by everyone.
Where visual communication is unsuitable, equivalent accessible alternatives should be provided.

### V. Formal Safety and Verification Rules

The proposed system requires more than a list of gestures. It needs explicit rules governing the limits of their interpretation.

Rule 1 — Recognition is not authorization
Recognizing a gesture does not automatically authorize a particular action.
[ \text{Recognized}(g) \not\Rightarrow \text{Authorized}(a) ]
The response must remain subject to context and appropriate safety requirements.

Rule 2 — A warning is not a diagnosis
A possible-hazard signal communicates a concern. It does not prove the presence, identity, or location of a specific hazard.
[ \text{Warning} \neq \text{Confirmed Hazard} ]
Nevertheless, uncertainty must not be used to dismiss a credible safety concern.
For suspected mines or explosive ordnance, civilians should not approach, touch, move, or investigate the object. They should keep away and use appropriate established warning and reporting channels. Consult the United Nations Mine Action Service's current explosive-ordnance risk education guidance.

Rule 3 — Arrival is not an all-clear
A person may reach a location without establishing that the location is safe.
[ \text{Arrival Confirmed} \neq \text{Safety Confirmed} ]
A status message should communicate only what is actually known.

Rule 4 — Conflicting signals require clarification
When signals conflict, the system must preserve the conflict rather than invent a resolution.
Clarification should be sought when safe and practicable. Where immediate danger exists, people should follow applicable emergency instructions rather than wait for a complete communication sequence.

Rule 5 — No gesture guarantees protection
The framework cannot guarantee that a gesture will be seen, understood, transmitted, or acted upon correctly.
The system must not be represented as a substitute for appropriate emergency planning, training, professional assistance, or official instructions.

Rule 6 — Human dignity constrains implementation
The proposed system must not be used to compel unnecessary movement, exclude people from assistance, or justify discriminatory treatment.
Children, older people, and people with disabilities should participate in the design and evaluation of communication methods that affect them.
These requirements align with the humanitarian emphasis on civilian protection and disability-inclusive emergency planning described by the ICRC and UNICEF.

### VI. Accessibility and Civilian-Centred Design

A civilian communication system is useful only if the people who need it can access and understand it.
A hand gesture may be unsuitable for a person with limited hand mobility, impaired vision, cognitive or communication differences, or an unfamiliarity with the teaching language.
A gesture visible to one person may be invisible to another.
Consequently, accessibility cannot be postponed until after the system has been designed.

6.1 Inclusive participation
The proposed framework recommends consultation with:
Children and their caregivers.
Older people.
People with disabilities and their representative organizations.
Community members who speak different languages.
People responsible for community health and welfare.
Qualified humanitarian and emergency-management personnel.
Participation should be voluntary, respectful, and appropriate to each person's circumstances.

6.2 Equivalent communication options
Depending on the context, alternatives may include spoken language, written messages, pictograms, agreed visual cards, accessible communication devices, or assistance from a trusted interpreter.
No single alternative should be assumed to work for everyone.

6.3 Protecting autonomy
A request for assistance is not permission to take control of another person's decisions.
Where possible, assistance should be offered in a manner that respects informed preferences, personal dignity, family relationships, and safeguarding requirements.
Protecting vulnerable people means including them in decision-making rather than treating them solely as objects to be moved or managed.

### VII. Human Oversight and the SSISM Mandatory Lockout Principle

SSISM emphasizes that a system must recognize when available evidence is insufficient to support a confident conclusion.
In the proposed communication framework, this becomes a requirement to preserve uncertainty and prevent an ambiguous signal from automatically authorizing a consequential action.

7.1 Conceptual decision states
A future implementation could distinguish among the following states:
NORMAL COMMUNICATION
A signal has been recognized and its intended meaning is sufficiently clear for the relevant low-risk communication task.
CLARIFICATION REQUIRED
The signal's meaning, sender, or context remains uncertain.
CONFLICT REVIEW
Two or more messages appear inconsistent and require appropriate resolution.
EMERGENCY RESPONSE
An immediate protective response is required under established emergency guidance.
REVIEW REQUIRED
New information may change an earlier interpretation or decision.
These are proposed conceptual states, not an implemented or validated control system.

7.2 Lockout must not mean universal immobility
The principle of pausing an uncertain decision can be useful, but a universal freeze rule could create danger in some emergencies.
The correct requirement is therefore:
Do not allow an ambiguous gesture, by itself, to authorize a consequential action.
This is different from requiring everyone to remain stationary until a particular signal is received.
Where immediate protective action is required, people should follow applicable emergency guidance and the instructions of appropriate emergency personnel.
The safety objective is not inaction at all costs. It is avoiding unjustified actions while preserving the ability to respond appropriately to genuine emergencies.

7.3 The Digital Trust Score is not a civilian safety certification
Earlier SSISM work proposed a Digital Trust Score, represented by (\Phi), and a lockout threshold.
That concept must not be transferred automatically into a physical emergency setting.
A numerical trust score cannot establish that a shelter, route, person, or location is safe unless the underlying variables, assumptions, evidence, and decision rules have been independently validated for that specific purpose.
For BEJ 82, the appropriate initial approach is qualitative and auditable: document what was observed, what was confirmed, what remains unknown, and who made the relevant decision.
Any future numerical model would require its own research and validation.

### VIII. Research Methodology and Validation Plan

The present manuscript is a conceptual specification.
Its publication does not establish that the proposed signals are effective, universally understood, or safe to use during an actual emergency.
A future study should evaluate the framework through a staged and ethically reviewed research process.

Stage A — Content and safety review
Seek review from relevant specialists in civilian protection, emergency communication, accessibility, safeguarding, and humanitarian risk education.
The purpose is to identify unsuitable meanings, unsafe assumptions, and conflicts with established emergency instructions before testing gestures with participants.

Stage B — Comprehension study
In a safe, controlled setting, assess whether participants can correctly interpret the proposed signals after a standardized teaching session.
Participants should be able to decline participation or withdraw without penalty.
The study should include appropriate representation of intended users and accessible participation methods.

Stage C — Ambiguity and failure analysis
Assess whether different gestures are confused, whether participants assign inconsistent meanings, and whether the absence of a signal is misinterpreted.
Testing should not expose participants to real hazards, simulated panic, or dangerous movement conditions.

Stage D — Accessibility evaluation
Evaluate the usability of the teaching materials and alternative communication methods with participants who have different communication and accessibility requirements.

Stage E — Independent analysis
Document the study design, participant selection, limitations, scoring rules, results, and any changes made to the framework.
Where feasible, an independent reviewer should assess the methods and reproduce the reported calculations.

Stage F — Revision and controlled retesting
Revise the proposed vocabulary in response to observed confusion or accessibility barriers.
Any subsequent evaluation should determine whether the revisions actually improve comprehension rather than assuming that additional complexity improves performance.

8.1 Proposed research metrics

The following are candidate measures for future testing. No results are claimed here.
Recognition accuracy
[ R_a = \frac{\text{Correctly recognized signals}} {\text{Signals presented}} ]
Interpretation accuracy
[ I_a = \frac{\text{Correctly interpreted meanings}} {\text{Signals assessed}} ]
Confusion rate
[ C_r = \frac{\text{Incorrect or ambiguous interpretations}} {\text{Interpretations assessed}} ]
Accessibility coverage
The proportion of intended participants for whom an appropriate and usable communication method has been evaluated.
Decision traceability
The proportion of assessed decisions for which the relevant signal, interpretation, uncertainty, and decision rationale are documented.
These measures require carefully defined denominators, participant-level analysis, and appropriate uncertainty estimates.
For example, repeated observations from the same participant should not automatically be treated as statistically independent observations.
A high recognition score would not, by itself, establish that a protocol reduces harm in the field.

8.2 Research questions

Future research should investigate:
Can intended users learn and consistently distinguish the proposed signals?
Which signals produce the most interpretation errors?
Do alternative communication methods improve accessibility?
Can participants distinguish a reported status from a verified safety conclusion?
How do people respond when a signal is missing, unclear, or contradicted?
Which design choices minimize confusion without creating false confidence?
Can independent researchers reproduce the reported results?
These questions provide a testable research programme without overstating what the current manuscript establishes.

### IX. Limitations and Conditions of Use

Several limitations must remain explicit.
First, the vocabulary is proposed rather than standardized. The meanings of the fifteen signals have not been established as universal conventions.
Second, the framework has not been empirically validated. No measured recognition rates, casualty-reduction estimates, or operational success rates are presented.
Third, communication is context-dependent. A gesture may have different meanings across communities, languages, age groups, and circumstances.
Fourth, accessibility is not automatic. Some people may be unable to see, produce, or interpret a particular gesture.
Fifth, safety cannot be inferred from communication alone. A signal does not establish that a route, shelter, or destination is safe.
Sixth, the framework is not an emergency command authority. It cannot replace official instructions, qualified humanitarian assistance, professional medical care, or specialist hazard guidance.
Seventh, the proposed mathematical expressions are conceptual research tools. They do not constitute a validated predictive model or a guarantee of safe outcomes.
Accordingly, BEJ 82 should be used at this stage for research, discussion, and controlled educational evaluation—not as a certified operational protocol during active danger.

### X. Research Integrity and Reproducibility

SSISM treats research integrity as more than the preservation of a document.
A reproducible research record should distinguish among document integrity, source reliability, methodological quality, and empirical validity.
10.1 Four separate verification questions
Document integrity: Does the file match its recorded checksum?
Source integrity: Can a reader identify and inspect the sources supporting the claims?
Methodological integrity: Are the definitions, assumptions, procedures, and calculations sufficiently explicit for independent assessment?
Empirical validity: Do appropriately collected observations support the conclusions being drawn?
These questions are related but not interchangeable.
A cryptographic checksum can help establish that a file matches a recorded reference. It cannot establish that the paper's arguments are correct or that a proposed safety framework works in practice.
10.2 Recommended publication record
The repository should preserve:
The manuscript version and publication date.
The Git commit identifier.
The SHA-256 checksum of the exact published file.
The sources used to ground the research.
A record of substantive revisions.
Any future study protocol and results, clearly distinguished from the conceptual manuscript.
The identity or description of independent reviewers, where disclosure is appropriate and consent has been obtained.
A verification record should be generated from the final file after editing. Previous checksums must not be carried forward as if they verify a revised version.
10.3 Publication status
For this revision, the appropriate status is:
FORMALLY SPECIFIED — RESEARCH VALIDATION PENDING
The terms verified, validated, field-tested, and proven effective should be reserved for claims supported by the relevant evidence.

### XI. Conclusion: Community Intelligence as Responsible Communication

BEJ 82 proposes that community intelligence can be understood, in part, as the capacity to communicate needs, recognize uncertainty, preserve human dignity, and make decisions without confusing a signal with proof.
The fifteen proposed signals provide a starting vocabulary for research into accessible civilian communication.
Their value cannot be established by the number of gestures, the sophistication of the terminology, or the apparent completeness of the formal specification.
Their value must ultimately depend on whether intended users understand them, whether their meanings remain clear under appropriate testing, whether they include people with different needs, and whether the framework avoids encouraging unsafe conclusions.
The central SSISM commitment remains:
Observation must remain distinct from interpretation.
Interpretation must remain distinct from confirmation.
Communication must remain distinct from authorization.
Document integrity must remain distinct from scientific validity.
Human dignity must constrain system design.
The purpose of Stage 4 is to specify a framework with explicit assumptions, boundaries, and failure states.
The purpose of the next research stage is to test those assumptions and revise the framework in response to evidence.
This paper therefore makes a limited but meaningful claim: a structured civilian communication framework has been proposed, its conceptual boundaries have been documented, and a pathway for independent evaluation has been defined.
It does not claim that the framework has already been shown to reduce harm.
Reality Before Narrative. Verification Before Certainty. Human Dignity Before System Performance.
May the use of knowledge support understanding, protect vulnerable people, and reduce avoidable harm.
Sabbe sattā sukhitā hontu — May all beings be happy and safe.
